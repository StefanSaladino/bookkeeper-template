Bookkeeping basics

What should monthly bookkeeping actually include?

A practical checklist for comparing monthly bookkeeping services and keeping your business ready for tax time.

By Maya Bennett6 min readUpdated August 16, 2026

A good monthly bookkeeping service does more than sort transactions. It creates a repeatable close process that turns activity from the previous month into accurate records, useful reports and a short list of decisions that need your attention.

The exact scope will depend on your business, but the following checklist gives you a strong baseline when comparing proposals or reviewing your current arrangement.

1. Every balance-sheet account is reconciled

Reconciliation means comparing the balance in your bookkeeping system with an independent statement or supporting record. At minimum, this normally includes:

  • Business bank and savings accounts
  • Business credit cards and lines of credit
  • Loan balances and owner contributions
  • Payment processors such as Stripe, Square or PayPal
  • Payroll liabilities and sales-tax accounts

Simply importing bank-feed transactions is not reconciliation. A proper review confirms that the opening balance, monthly activity and ending balance agree—and investigates any difference.

2. Revenue and expenses are categorized consistently

Categories should reflect how the business actually operates. Consistency matters because month-to-month comparisons become unreliable when the same expense is coded differently each time.

Your bookkeeper should also separate personal or shareholder activity, identify capital purchases, and ask before guessing about unusual transactions. A short monthly questions list is much more efficient than a large year-end cleanup.

3. Receivables and payables are reviewed

If you invoice customers or enter supplier bills, the monthly close should include an accounts-receivable and accounts-payable review. Old balances, duplicate invoices, unapplied credits and payments posted to the wrong customer can quietly distort cash-flow decisions.

Useful monthly question: Which customer invoices are overdue, and which supplier payments will affect cash over the next 30 days?

4. Payroll and sales-tax records agree

Payroll reports should reconcile with wages, source deductions and employer costs recorded in the general ledger. GST/HST collected and paid should also flow into the correct accounts so the return can be prepared from defensible records.

Your bookkeeper may or may not file payroll and sales-tax returns, but the proposal should clearly state who is responsible, what deadlines apply and how confirmation of filing will be stored.

5. You receive a useful reporting package

A basic monthly package normally includes a profit-and-loss statement and balance sheet. Many service businesses also benefit from a cash summary, receivables aging, comparison with prior periods, or revenue by service line.

The reports should arrive on an agreed schedule and include enough context to help you use them. At a minimum, you should understand:

  • what changed materially from the previous month;
  • whether cash, receivables or expenses require attention;
  • which estimates or unresolved items could affect the numbers; and
  • what information is still needed from you.

6. The books are year-end ready

Monthly work should make year-end easier, not postpone every difficult item. Ask whether the service includes a year-end package for your accountant, supporting schedules, document organization and responses to reasonable follow-up questions.

A clear proposal should also state what is outside scope—such as corporate tax returns, assurance work, financial planning or complex inventory accounting. That protects both sides and helps you assemble the right professional team.

The simplest test

By the end of each monthly close, you should be able to trust the balances, understand the important movements and know what needs action. If you only receive a set of statements without confidence or context, the process is not finished.
