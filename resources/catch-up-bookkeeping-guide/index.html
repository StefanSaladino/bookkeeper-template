Catch-up guide

Behind on your books? Start here—not with panic.

A clear step-by-step plan for organizing overdue bookkeeping, protecting filed periods and building a sustainable monthly process.

By Maya Bennett7 min readUpdated August 16, 2026

Falling behind on bookkeeping is common. Growth, staff changes, software migrations and simple lack of time can all create a backlog. The safest response is a controlled cleanup: define the period, gather reliable records and work account by account.

Step 1: Define the cleanup period

Identify the last month that was fully reconciled and reviewed. If prior GST/HST, payroll or income-tax filings have already been submitted, note those dates before changing historical records.

The cleanup scope should specify a clear starting date, ending date, list of accounts and expected deliverables. This prevents the project from expanding every time a new issue appears.

Step 2: Gather records before categorizing

Complete source documents reduce guessing and rework. Collect:

  • Bank and credit-card statements for every month in scope
  • Loan, line-of-credit and payment-processor statements
  • Sales invoices, supplier bills and major purchase documents
  • Payroll registers and remittance confirmations
  • GST/HST returns and notices already filed
  • The previous accountant's year-end adjusting entries, if available

Bank-feed access helps, but statements remain important because they provide the independent ending balances needed for reconciliation.

Step 3: Protect previously filed periods

Do not casually recategorize transactions in a closed year or filed sales-tax period. Changes can alter amounts that were already reported. Record the issue, determine whether it is material and coordinate with the appropriate accountant or tax professional before amending anything.

Important: A catch-up project should preserve an audit trail. Keep notes describing significant corrections, source documents used and the person who approved any treatment that required judgment.

Step 4: Reconcile from the balance sheet outward

A strong cleanup normally starts with cash, credit cards, loans, receivables, payables, payroll liabilities and sales tax. Once those balances are supported, revenue and expenses can be reviewed with much greater confidence.

Uncategorized transactions should be grouped into one organized questions list. Include the date, vendor, amount, account and a short explanation of what is needed. Sending questions in batches is faster for everyone.

Step 5: Review the completed period

Before declaring the backlog complete, review comparative profit-and-loss statements, the balance sheet and key control accounts. Look for unusual negative balances, duplicate revenue, stale receivables, uncategorized assets and owner transactions posted as ordinary expenses.

The final package should tell you:

  • which months and accounts were reconciled;
  • which assumptions or unresolved items remain;
  • whether tax or payroll filings may require professional review; and
  • what information your year-end accountant will receive.

Step 6: Prevent the next backlog

The cleanup is only valuable if the new process is sustainable. Choose one receipt-capture tool, connect business-only accounts, set a monthly close date and define who answers bookkeeping questions.

A lightweight monthly routine is usually enough: documents submitted weekly, questions answered once, accounts reconciled monthly and reports reviewed on a predictable date.

When to bring in help

Professional help is especially useful when several years are involved, records disagree with filed returns, payroll balances are unclear, inventory is material, or personal and business spending are heavily mixed. A good bookkeeper can organize the records and coordinate with your accountant without pretending that every issue is purely clerical.
