Financial clarity

Five numbers every service business should review monthly

Five practical financial indicators that help service businesses monitor cash, margins, collections and sustainable growth.

By Maya Bennett5 min readUpdated August 16, 2026

Financial reports become useful when they lead to better questions. For most service businesses, you do not need dozens of ratios every month. You need a small set of reliable numbers that reveal whether the company is collecting, delivering and spending in a healthy way.

1. Available operating cash

Start with the cash the business can actually use—not the total bank balance if part of it is reserved for GST/HST, payroll deductions or income tax. Comparing available cash with the next four to eight weeks of expected obligations gives you a more honest view of flexibility.

Ask: After protected tax and payroll funds are removed, how many weeks of normal operating costs could the business cover?

2. Accounts receivable and average collection time

Revenue on the profit-and-loss statement does not pay bills until the customer pays you. Review total receivables, invoices over 30 days and the average number of days between invoicing and collection.

A rising receivables balance can signal unclear payment terms, inconsistent follow-up or reliance on one slow customer. The earlier you notice the pattern, the easier it is to correct.

3. Gross profit or delivery margin

Gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the work. For a service business, direct costs might include subcontractors, project labour, merchant fees, materials or software used specifically for client delivery.

Looking only at revenue can hide unprofitable growth. If sales increase while delivery margin shrinks, pricing, scope control or staffing may need attention.

Simple calculation: Gross margin percentage = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100. Use the same cost definitions each month so the trend remains meaningful.

4. Operating profit

Operating profit shows what remains after ordinary delivery and overhead costs, before items such as corporate income tax and certain financing costs. It helps answer whether the core business model is supporting the team, systems and owner compensation it requires.

Compare the current month with the same period last year and with a rolling three-month average. A single unusual month may not mean much; a trend usually does.

5. Tax and payroll amounts set aside

Sales tax collected, payroll source deductions and income-tax reserves are not normal spending money. Track the estimated obligation and confirm that enough cash has been separated to cover it.

For income tax, your accountant can help establish an appropriate reserve based on profitability and instalment requirements. Your bookkeeper can then make that reserve visible in the monthly process.

How to make the review sustainable

Choose one day each month, use a consistent one-page summary and write down two or three decisions. The goal is not to become an accountant. It is to notice changes while there is still time to respond.

  • Use finalized, reconciled numbers—not a live bank feed that is still changing.
  • Compare trends, not isolated amounts.
  • Attach an owner or deadline to each follow-up.
  • Ask your bookkeeper when a number does not make sense.
